Part 1 · Foundations

What is Database Activity Monitoring?

Database Activity Monitoring (DAM) is a security practice — and a category of tools — that watches everything happening inside your databases in real time: who connected, what queries they ran, what data they read or changed, and whether any of it looks risky.

Think of your database as a bank vault holding your most valuable asset: data — customer records, payment details, health information, intellectual property. A firewall guards the building's front door, but once someone is inside, who is watching what they actually do with the vault? That's DAM. It's the CCTV + alarm system for your data.

👁️

Visibility

A complete record of every login, query, and data access across all your databases.

🚨

Detection

Automatic alerts when someone does something unusual or against the rules.

🛡️

Response

The ability to block, kill, or quarantine a risky session before damage is done.

📋

Compliance

Tamper-proof audit trails that prove to regulators you're watching your data.

In one sentence: DAM answers the question "Who is doing what to my data, right now — and is any of it dangerous?"
Part 1 · Foundations

Why does DAM matter?

1. The biggest threats are often inside the network

Traditional security (firewalls, VPNs) protects the perimeter. But data breaches frequently come from credentials that are already trusted: a compromised app account, a curious employee, a departing DBA copying the customer list, or an attacker who stole a password. To them the database says "come in." DAM is what notices the bulk download of 2 million customer records at 3 a.m. — because behaviour, not just identity, is being watched.

2. Compliance requires it

Most data-protection regulations legally require you to monitor and log access to sensitive data:

RegulationProtectsWhat it expects of you
GDPREU personal dataTrack access to personal data; support data-subject requests; report breaches fast.
PCI-DSSCard / payment dataLog all access to cardholder data; monitor privileged users.
HIPAAUS health recordsAudit who viewed patient data and when.
SOXFinancial reportingProve the integrity and controls around financial data.

3. When something goes wrong, you need the story

After an incident, the first questions are: What was accessed? By whom? How much? When? Without DAM you're guessing. With it, you have a precise, time-stamped, tamper-evident record — the difference between "we contained it" and "we have no idea."

The stakes: the average data breach costs millions and takes months to even detect. DAM's whole purpose is to collapse that detection time from months to minutes.

Key terms you'll meet

You don't need to memorise these — they'll click as you go — but here's a friendly map. (Full glossary at the end.)

Principal
The "who" — a user or service account that connects to a database (e.g. app_crm, dba_mueller).
Query
A command sent to the database. SELECT reads data; INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE change it; GRANT changes permissions.
Session
One connection from a principal to a database, from login to logout — the container for all their queries.
Sensitive data / PII
Data that must be protected — names, emails, card numbers, tax IDs, health records. PII = Personally Identifiable Information.
Policy / Rule
A condition that defines "risky." E.g. "alert if anyone reads more than 10,000 rows of PII."
Alert
What's raised when activity matches a policy — the thing a security analyst investigates.
Quarantine
The response: isolating or blocking a risky account/session so it can't do (more) harm.
Agent
The lightweight software that actually captures the database activity and feeds it to SecurEra.

Stage 1 — Capturing activity

Before you can monitor anything, you have to see it. DAM tools capture database activity in a few ways; SecurEra supports the main ones:

Beginner takeaway: a database SecurEra doesn't capture is a database it can't protect. Step one is always getting monitoring onto your data sources.

Stage 2 & 3 — Analyse and detect with policies

Raw activity is just noise until you decide what counts as risky. That's the job of policies (also called rules). A policy is a plain-language condition, for example:

  • "Bulk read of sensitive data" — someone reads an unusually large number of PII rows.
  • "Privileged off-hours access" — a DBA touches personal data at 3 a.m.
  • "Credential brute force" — many failed logins from one source.
  • "GRANT of DBA / SYSDBA" — someone hands out top-level privileges.

Enabled vs Monitor vs Disabled

A crucial concept for beginners — every policy has a state:

StateWhat happensWhen to use
EnabledMatches raise real alerts.You trust the rule.
Monitor (shadow)Matches are counted but no alert fires.Testing a new rule to see if it's noisy before it wakes anyone up.
DisabledThe rule never runs.Not relevant to you.
Best practice: put a brand-new rule in Monitor first. Watch it for a few days. If it only fires on genuinely bad things, promote it to Enabled. This is how you avoid "alert fatigue."

Behavioral analytics — catching what rules can't (UEBA)

Policies are powerful, but they only catch threats you can describe in advance. Many real attacks — a stolen login, a rogue insider, a service account gone wrong — look like perfectly ordinary queries. Nothing about a single SELECT screams "breach." What gives it away is that it's unusual for that person.

UEBA (User & Entity Behavior Analytics) is the part of DAM that learns what "normal" looks like for every user and service account, then flags activity that deviates from it — without you writing a rule.

Step 1 — It learns a baseline for each "entity"

An entity is anyone (or anything) that touches your databases — a human like dba_mueller, or a service account like bi_reader. For each one, DAM quietly learns, from a few days of ordinary activity:

  • When they're usually active — the hours and days they normally work.
  • How much they usually read — their typical and peak result sizes.
  • What they usually touch — the tables that are normal for them.

That learned picture is their baseline. On the Behavioral Analytics page you can see it as a heatmap of an entity's normal hours — darker cells are busier.

Step 2 — It scores deviations from that baseline

Once an entity has a baseline, DAM watches for behavior that doesn't fit it. Three signals matter most:

🌙

Off-normal-hours

Activity outside the hours this entity normally works — especially against sensitive data. e.g. an analyst who only ever works 9–5 pulls customer PII at 3 a.m.

📈

Volume anomaly

A result set far larger than this entity's own norm. e.g. a service account that averages ~100 rows suddenly returns 60,000.

🆕

First-time access

Touching a table the entity has never accessed before. e.g. a payroll app account suddenly reading a salaries table it has never queried.

Each entity gets a rolling risk score from 0 to 100 that blends these signals (plus any open alerts against them). So instead of only asking "which database is risky?", you can now ask "which user is behaving strangely right now?"

Rules vs UEBA — you want both

Policies (rules)UEBA (behavior)
CatchesKnown-bad patterns you can describeUnusual behavior you didn't predict
You defineThe condition (e.g. "> 10,000 PII rows")Nothing — it learns the normal itself
Best atClear policy & compliance linesStolen credentials, insider drift, misused service accounts
Worked example. bi_reader normally runs a few hundred report queries on weekday afternoons. One Saturday at 02:14 it opens a table it has never touched and exports 48,000 rows. No single query breaks a rule — but it's off-hours, a volume spike, and first-time access all at once. UEBA scores bi_reader as high-risk and raises a behavioral alert — before the data is gone.

Where to see it

Open Behavioral Analytics in the sidebar (under Threats). Your entities are ranked by risk, each with chips showing why (off-hours, volume, new tables). Click any entity to drill in: its learned-hours heatmap, the signals driving its score, and its recent alerts and activity.

Give it a few days. UEBA needs to watch normal activity before it can spot the abnormal — a brand-new entity has no baseline yet, so it is not over-flagged. Baselines also adapt over time, so as legitimate work patterns change, "normal" moves with them.

Stage 4 — Alerts and severity

When enabled-policy conditions are met, DAM raises an alert. Not every alert is equally urgent, so each carries a severity that tells your team what to look at first:

● Critical ● High ● Medium ● Low

A healthy workflow treats alerts like a queue: acknowledge that you've seen it, investigate what happened, then resolve it (or escalate/quarantine if it's real). Alerts you decide are harmless can be marked a false positive, which teaches the system to stop bugging you about that pattern.

A false positive is an alert that turned out to be benign. Tuning them down is a normal, healthy part of running DAM — not a failure.

Stage 5 — Responding to threats

Detection without response is just a very good alarm nobody acts on. When an alert is a genuine threat, DAM lets you act:

⛔

Quarantine an account

Block a principal so it can't open new sessions.

✂️

Terminate a session

Kill the live connection immediately, mid-query.

↗️

Escalate

Hand it to on-call / senior responders.

🎯

Active defense

Plant decoys (fake data) to catch and study attackers.

Quarantine has two flavours you'll use often: Terminate ends the live session but keeps the account blocked, while Release lifts a quarantine once you've confirmed things are fine.

Knowing what to protect — classification & compliance

You can't prioritise protecting data if you don't know where the sensitive stuff lives. Classification scans your databases and labels columns that hold PII — emails, card numbers, tax IDs — so policies and reports can focus on what actually matters.

Related tools you'll see in SecurEra:

  • Masking — hide or scramble sensitive values (e.g. show ****1234 instead of a full card number) so people can work without seeing raw PII.
  • Access Governance — control who is allowed to reach which data, including just-in-time (JIT) access that's granted temporarily and auto-expires.
  • Compliance Center, DSAR & Audit Trail — prove you're doing all of the above: posture against GDPR/PCI/HIPAA/SOX, handle "what data do you hold on me?" requests, and keep an immutable log.
The Audit Trail is deliberately tamper-evident — even admins can't quietly rewrite history. That's what makes it trustworthy to a regulator.

Assessing the database itself — Vulnerability Assessment

Everything so far watches activity — who did what, and whether it looked risky. Vulnerability Assessment (VA) asks a different question: is the database itself configured securely? It's the difference between the CCTV watching the vault (monitoring) and an inspector checking that the vault door, locks and alarms are actually up to standard (posture).

A VA scan logs into the database as a read-only reader and runs a library of checks derived from the CIS Database Benchmarks — the industry-standard hardening baselines. Nothing is changed and nothing is exploited; it only reads configuration and catalog settings. Typical checks include:

  • Misconfiguration — auditing turned off, TLS not enforced, risky features like local_infile left on, insecure defaults.
  • Privilege & access — excessive grants, PUBLIC/wildcard-host grants, unused or default accounts, too many superusers.
  • Authentication — empty or default passwords, deprecated auth plugins, weak password-lifetime policy.
  • Encryption — at-rest encryption, TLS version and ciphers, secure-transport enforcement.

Each check produces a finding with a result (pass, fail, or error if the reader can't see that setting), a severity, the evidence (the actual value found), and remediation (how to fix it). Findings roll up into a single posture score — the severity-weighted percentage of checks that passed — so you can track hardening over time.

Scope, honestly. VA here means database security posture — the engine and instance. It is not OS/host CIS, network port scanning, or web-app scanning, and it doesn't exploit anything. It's read-only, authenticated assessment, the same category as Oracle DBSAT or the CIS Database Benchmarks.
VA and Classification take the same read-only path into the database, so a monitoring agent that already has a DB login can run scans with no extra plumbing. VA findings also feed the Compliance Center — a database with open critical findings, or one that hasn't been scanned recently, shows up as a compliance gap.

Part 2 · How DAM works

Deploying a discovery agent

Discovery finds databases you don't already know about. Network discovery is run by an in-network discovery agent — a scanner with reachability to your database subnets. It sweeps a range of addresses, identifies each open port by protocol handshake (so a database on a non-default port is still found), and reports candidates back over outbound HTTPS. You Approve a candidate to register it; monitoring is deployed separately.

Where it runs. Put one agent in a hub VPC that is VPC-peered to each VNet holding databases — the hub reaches every peered spoke, so you don't need an agent in every app VPC. Peering requires non-overlapping CIDRs. Managed / PaaS databases (RDS, Cloud SQL, Azure SQL) sit behind non-transitive service peerings and are found by cloud-API discovery (Cloud connectors) instead — no network path needed.
  1. Place it with network line-of-sight. Deploy on a host that can reach the DB subnets on their ports. Allow its IP on DB ports from the spokes' firewalls; the agent itself needs only outbound HTTPS to the control plane — nothing inbound.
  2. Use this workspace's enroll token. Copy the per-tenant token from Discovery → How to deploy a discovery agent (or Agents & Coverage → Deploy monitoring). A shared/global token would enroll the agent into the wrong tenant, and it would never appear on your Discovery page.
  3. Install & run the agent — it's a dependency-free Node service (Node 18+, for global fetch): 
    # 1) Node 20+
curl -fsSL https://deb.nodesource.com/setup_20.x | sudo bash - && sudo apt-get install -y nodejs

# 2) put the agent source (dev/dam/discovery/*.js) in /opt/toovix-discovery, then:
sudo tee /etc/toovix-discovery.env >/dev/null <<'EOF'
CONTROL_PLANE=https://<your-dam-host>
AGENT_ENROLL_TOKEN=tvxenr_<this-workspace>
DISCOVERY_TARGETS=10.10.0.0/24,10.20.0.0/24   # your VNet CIDRs / hosts / ranges
DISCOVERY_PRESET=common
DISCOVERY_INTERVAL=300000
EOF

# 3) run under systemd (restarts on crash, survives reboot)
sudo systemctl enable --now toovix-discovery
  4. Point it at your CIDRs. DISCOVERY_TARGETS accepts hostnames, single IPs, CIDR blocks (10.40.0.0/24) and ranges (10.50.0.10-40), comma-separated. Expansion is IPv4-only and capped at a /16 (override with DISCOVERY_MAX_HOSTS) so a fat prefix can't launch a million-host sweep.
  5. Verify. Within ~20 seconds (then every DISCOVERY_INTERVAL) it reports; on the Discovery page the status strip flips to agent online, Run scan enables, and discovered databases appear as candidates.
GCP shortcut. The dev/enterprise-test/terraform/discovery-hub.tf module builds the whole thing — hub VPC, star peering, Cloud NAT, firewall, and the scanner VM with the agent baked in. Set agent_enroll_token + dam_control_plane_url and terraform apply.
Gotchas, in order of how often they bite: wrong token (agent lands in the wrong tenant) · firewall blocks the agent from DB ports (0 found) · overlapping CIDRs (peering refuses them) · Node < 18 (no global fetch).
Step 2

Deploy monitoring

A registered database isn't watched until an agent is monitoring it. In the console you'd click Databases → Deploy monitoring (or Agents & Coverage → Deploy monitoring), pick a capture model, and paste the generated command onto your database host. The rest of this section is the full reference for what those commands do, per model and per engine.

Pick a capture model

SecurEra captures activity three ways, all feeding the same pipeline from Part 2. Which one applies depends on the engine and where it runs — a self-managed database (VM / on-prem) can use all three; a managed PaaS database you don't control can only use Agentless.

AgentLite · recommended

Reads the telemetry the database already produces — no wire tap, no path change. Reads after decryption, so it captures TLS and local/IPC sessions. Works on every engine. Detective only.

Agent

Taps the connection: network (passive), host eBPF (below TLS), or an inline proxy — the only mode that can block. Exact row counts. MySQL / PostgreSQL.

Agentless · PaaS

The cloud emits its native audit to a stream (Pub/Sub · Event Hub · Kinesis); DAM consumes it. Nothing installed — the only option for managed databases.

Step 2a · AgentLite

AgentLite — per engine

AgentLite reads the database's own telemetry and forwards each statement. Two shapes: a log tailer on the DB host (MySQL, PostgreSQL) and a remote poller over the DB protocol that runs anywhere and installs nothing on the DB host (SQL Server, Oracle, MongoDB).

🐬 MySQL / MariaDB — general query log

Write the general log to a fixed path, then run the agent tailing it:

-- as MySQL admin (runtime; persist in /etc/mysql/…/zz-toovix.cnf too)
SET GLOBAL log_output='FILE';
SET GLOBAL general_log_file='/var/log/mysql/general.log';
SET GLOBAL general_log='ON';
docker run -d --name toovix-agent --restart unless-stopped --user 0 \
  -v /var/log/mysql/general.log:/var/log/mysql/general.log:ro \
  -e MODE=audit-forward -e DB_ENGINE=mysql -e TARGET_PORT=3306 \
  -e AUDIT_SOURCE=general_log -e AUDIT_LOG=/var/log/mysql/general.log \
  -e TARGET_HOST=$DB_HOST \
  -e AGENT_ENROLL_TOKEN=$TOKEN -e CONTROL_PLANE=$CP  <dam-agent-image>

🐘 PostgreSQL — statement logging

Turn on statement logging with the exact prefix the parser expects, then reload (no restart):

ALTER SYSTEM SET log_statement='all';
ALTER SYSTEM SET log_line_prefix='%m [%p] %u@%d ';   -- prefix matters
SELECT pg_reload_conf();

Run the agent as MySQL above but with DB_ENGINE=postgresql, TARGET_PORT=5432, AUDIT_SOURCE=pgaudit, and AUDIT_LOG=/var/log/postgresql/postgresql-16-main.log (match your PG version). Built-in logging — pgaudit extension not required.

🪟 SQL Server — polled over TDS (nothing on Windows)

Binary telemetry, so AgentLite polls over TDS — the collector is a Linux container reaching port 1433; nothing installs on Windows. Two sources: SQL Server Audit (clean object scoping, no row counts) or Extended Events (adds row counts). Extended Events:

CREATE EVENT SESSION ToovixXE ON SERVER
  ADD EVENT sqlserver.sql_statement_completed (
    ACTION (sqlserver.server_principal_name, sqlserver.client_hostname)
    WHERE ([sqlserver].[database_name]=N'YourDB'))
  ADD TARGET package0.event_file (SET filename=N'C:\SQLAudit\ToovixXE.xel', max_file_size=50)
  WITH (MAX_DISPATCH_LATENCY=5 SECONDS, STARTUP_STATE=ON);
ALTER EVENT SESSION ToovixXE ON SERVER STATE = START;   -- START, not ON
docker run -d --name toovix-agent-mssql --restart unless-stopped \
  -e MODE=audit-forward -e DB_ENGINE=mssql \
  -e TARGET_HOST=10.20.2.4 -e TARGET_PORT=1433 -e DB_NAME=YourDB \
  -e AUDIT_SOURCE=xevents -e AUDIT_LOG='C:\SQLAudit\ToovixXE*.xel' \
  -e DB_USER=dam_svc -e DB_PASSWORD=<pw> \
  -e AGENT_ENROLL_TOKEN=$TOKEN -e CONTROL_PLANE=$CP  <dam-agent-image>
DB login needed (reads over TDS, not disk): CONTROL SERVER for Audit or VIEW SERVER STATE for XEvents. Azure SQL (PaaS) can't host a collector — use Agentless.
SQL Server Express works too (and Developer / Web / Standard) — capture is the same on every edition, verified end-to-end. Use the XEvents source (Extended Events is core-engine everywhere; SQL Server Audit needs 2016 SP1+). Three Express-only defaults to fix first: enable TCP/IP (off by default — the collector polls over TDS), pin a static port (Express installs as the named instance HOST\SQLEXPRESS on a dynamic port; the collector connects by host:port), and mind the 10 GB/DB · ~1 GB pool caps so the .xel ring stays small. No SQL Server Agent needed — the collector tails the file itself. Full checklist in the Onboarding SOP § SQL Server.

🔴 Oracle — Unified Auditing over SQL*Net

Polls the UNIFIED_AUDIT_TRAIL view over SQL*Net — the collector runs on any Linux host reaching 1521, covering Oracle on a VM, on RDS, and on OCI. Enable a policy and create the read-only login:

CREATE AUDIT POLICY toovix_dml ACTIONS SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE;
AUDIT POLICY toovix_dml BY appuser;   -- scope to the app schema owner

CREATE USER dam_svc IDENTIFIED BY "<pw>";
GRANT CREATE SESSION, AUDIT_VIEWER, SELECT_CATALOG_ROLE TO dam_svc;
docker run -d --name toovix-agent-oracle --restart unless-stopped \
  -e MODE=audit-forward -e DB_ENGINE=oracle \
  -e TARGET_HOST=10.0.13.149 -e TARGET_PORT=1521 -e DB_NAME=FREEPDB1 \
  -e DB_USER=dam_svc -e DB_PASSWORD=<pw> \
  -e AGENT_ENROLL_TOKEN=$TOKEN -e CONTROL_PLANE=$CP  <dam-agent-image>
Row counts come from a join to V$SQLSTATS — exact for ad-hoc queries, a per-execution average for parameterised app traffic. AUDIT_VIEWER reads the trail; SELECT_CATALOG_ROLE covers V$SQLSTATS and classification. RDS Oracle and OCI Autonomous Database work through this same path — for ADB set TARGET_PORT=1522, pass the long service name in ORACLE_SERVICE, and the collector auto-enables one-way TLS (no wallet, verified against a live ADB). Set ORACLE_WALLET for the mutual-TLS/wallet path instead.

🍃 MongoDB — database profiler

Community MongoDB has no audit log, so AgentLite polls the built-in profiler collection system.profile over the wire — runs anywhere, covers Atlas and any remote mongod. Enable profiling and create a least-privilege login (a custom role with only enableProfiler — don't grant dbAdmin, which bundles dropCollection):

use <db>
db.setProfilingLevel(2, { slowms: 0 })   // every op; persist in mongod.conf
docker run -d --name toovix-agent-mongo --restart unless-stopped \
  -e MODE=audit-forward -e DB_ENGINE=mongodb -e AUDIT_SOURCE=profiler \
  -e TARGET_HOST=10.50.0.2 -e TARGET_PORT=27017 -e DB_NAME=<db> \
  -e DB_USER=dam_svc -e DB_PASSWORD=<pw> -e MONGO_AUTH_SOURCE=admin \
  -e AGENT_ENROLL_TOKEN=$TOKEN -e CONTROL_PLANE=$CP  <dam-agent-image>
Profiler limits: system.profile is capped (best-effort, not guaranteed under heavy load); row_count under-reports large reads unless MONGO_INCLUDE_GETMORE=true. Classification samples documents (no fixed schema to read).
Classification (which columns hold PII/PCI) runs alongside any AgentLite capture — add CLASSIFY=true, DB_USER/DB_PASSWORD, and DB_NAME (all engines except MySQL need the last). Reads information_schema (MySQL/PG/SQL Server), DBA_TAB_COLUMNS (Oracle), or samples documents (MongoDB).
Step 2b · Agent

Agent — network · host eBPF · inline proxy

The Agent taps the connection instead of the audit — for MySQL and PostgreSQL. It's how you get exact row counts on every event and, with the proxy, real-time blocking. All three run from the same image, selected by MODE.

MySQL / PostgreSQL only. Host eBPF hooks OpenSSL — SQL Server (SChannel) and Oracle (libnnz) can't be hooked, and Mongo wire-decode is low-value. For those, use AgentLite.

Network — passive wire capture

On the DB host, shares its network namespace. Zero path change, ~0 overhead. Cleartext only (TLS is opaque):

docker run -d --name toovix-agent-network --restart unless-stopped \
  --network host --cap-add NET_ADMIN --cap-add NET_RAW \
  -e MODE=network -e DB_ENGINE=mysql -e TARGET_PORT=3306 -e TARGET_HOST=$DB_HOST \
  -e AGENT_ENROLL_TOKEN=$TOKEN -e CONTROL_PLANE=$CP  <dam-agent-image>

Host (eBPF) — below TLS

Also on the DB host, but attaches eBPF uprobes to the database's libssl — reads TLS-encrypted sessions the network agent can't. Kernel 5.8+:

docker run -d --name toovix-agent-host --restart unless-stopped \
  --network host --pid host --privileged \
  -e MODE=host -e DB_ENGINE=postgresql -e TARGET_PORT=5432 -e TARGET_HOST=$DB_HOST \
  -e AGENT_ENROLL_TOKEN=$TOKEN -e CONTROL_PLANE=$CP  <dam-agent-image>
Deploy network + host together. They're complementary, not redundant: network reads cleartext (TLS is opaque to it), host reads encrypted (cleartext never calls SSL_read). Neither double-counts the other — this is the recommended "full visibility" posture for self-managed MySQL/PG.

Inline proxy — the only mode that can block

Listens on its own port, forwards to the real DB, and can drop or kill a session per policy. Clients connect to the proxy instead of the DB — the one mode with a path change:

docker run -d --name toovix-agent-proxy --restart unless-stopped -p 3307:3307 \
  -e MODE=proxy -e DB_ENGINE=mysql -e LISTEN_PORT=3307 -e UPSTREAM=<db>:3306 \
  -e TARGET_HOST=$DB_HOST \
  -e AGENT_ENROLL_TOKEN=$TOKEN -e CONTROL_PLANE=$CP  <dam-agent-image>
# apps now connect to the proxy on :3307 instead of the DB on :3306
Pair the proxy with a network agent so clients that bypass it are still seen. If the proxy→DB hop is cleartext, the network agent records that leg too with the proxy as the client IP — keep that hop on TLS.

For a managed database you don't control — RDS, Cloud SQL, Azure SQL, Cosmos — no agent can be installed. Instead the cloud emits the database's native audit to a managed stream, and the DAM connector consumes it. Setup is entirely cloud-side plus one connector in DAM.

🟦 Azure SQL → Event Hub (verified ~2-min end-to-end)

Enable database-level auditing to an Event Hub — it must be database-level; server-level with a DB-scoped diagnostic setting captures nothing. Then add an Azure Event Hub connector in DAM with the hub's listen connection string.

az sql db audit-policy update -g <RG> -s <server> -n <db> \
  --state Enabled --event-hub-target-state Enabled \
  --event-hub <hub> --event-hub-authorization-rule-id <send-rule-id> \
  --actions BATCH_COMPLETED_GROUP SUCCESSFUL_DATABASE_AUTHENTICATION_GROUP

🟨 GCP Cloud SQL (MySQL / PostgreSQL) → Pub/Sub (verified)

Enable the audit flags, route the logs to a Pub/Sub topic via a Cloud Logging sink, and let the DAM connector pull the subscription. Cloud SQL emits a structured audit proto (not text), which the connector normalizes.

# PostgreSQL example — pgaudit
gcloud sql instances patch <instance> \
  --database-flags=cloudsql.enable_pgaudit=on,pgaudit.log=read,write,ddl,role
gcloud pubsub topics create toovix-dam-audit
gcloud logging sinks create toovix-cloudsql-audit-sink \
  pubsub.googleapis.com/projects/<proj>/topics/toovix-dam-audit \
  --log-filter='resource.type="cloudsql_database" AND resource.labels.database_id="<proj>:<instance>"'
# grant the sink's writer identity roles/pubsub.publisher, then create a subscription

🟧 AWS RDS / Aurora → Kinesis (roadmap)

RDS publishes a Database Activity Stream (or exports audit logs to CloudWatch) which a subscription filter forwards to Kinesis. The Kinesis adapter is on the roadmap; for MySQL/PostgreSQL on RDS today, an AgentLite collector on a small EC2 box in the VPC is the interim path. RDS Oracle is reachable by the AgentLite Oracle collector via the master user.

🍃 MongoDB — Cosmos / Atlas (roadmap)

Azure Cosmos DB for MongoDB emits MongoRequests diagnostics to an Event Hub (transport exists; a normalizer is pending). MongoDB Atlas has no native push stream — its audit is a pull-based log export to S3. For managed MongoDB today, the AgentLite profiler poller can target a remote mongod including Atlas.

🔴 OCI Autonomous Database — covered by the poller

No cloud stream is used for OCI ADB. A true agentless pipeline on OCI would be ADB → Data Safe → an OCI Function → Logging → Service Connector Hub → OCI Streaming — five hops with custom code, to turn a pull into a push. It isn't worth it: the AgentLite Oracle collector already reaches managed ADB directly over one-way TLS (nothing installed on the ADB), with row counts and classification. That's the pragmatic agentless answer for OCI — verified end to end against a live Autonomous Database.

Once activity flows — by any model — you'll see it on the Dashboard and, if risky, in Alerts. Confirm the agent/connector is healthy under Agents & Coverage.
Step 3

Classify your sensitive data

Now find where the PII lives. Go to Classification.

  1. Click Run Scan. SecurEra inspects columns and labels sensitive ones (emails, card numbers, tax IDs, etc.).
  2. Review the results. Sensitive columns now carry tags that policies and reports can target.
  3. Want to hide sensitive values from certain users? Go to Masking and click Add to create a masking rule for a database.
Classification is what makes a rule like "bulk read of sensitive data" meaningful — the system now knows which data is sensitive.
Step 4

Tune your detection policies

Go to Policies & Rules. New workspaces start with a set of sensible default rules, so you're not beginning from scratch.

  1. Review the list. Each rule shows its current state.
  2. Set a rule's state:
    • ✓ Enable — raise real alerts on matches.
    • ◎ Move to Monitor — shadow mode: count matches silently while you evaluate the rule.
    • ○ Disable — turn the rule off.
  3. Create your own rule with New rule.
  4. Need to whitelist a known-good pattern? Use Add exception; remove one later with Revoke. Toggle Active / All to include revoked/expired items. Every "false positive" you mark on an alert also shows up here as a suppression — so this is where you revoke it if you want the rule to fire again.
Remember the golden rule from Part 2: new rules start in Monitor, graduate to Enable once proven quiet.
Step 5

Triage alerts

This is the day-to-day heartbeat of DAM. Go to Alerts.

  1. Work top-down by severity (Critical first). Click an alert to see the who / what / where / when.
  2. Move it through its lifecycle:
    • ✓ Acknowledge — "I've seen this, I'm on it." (✓ Acknowledge all clears a batch.)
    • ✓ Resolve — investigated and closed.
    • ↗ Escalate — hand to on-call.
    • ⎚ Session timeline — reconstruct the full session behind the alert.
  3. If it's a genuine threat, hit ⛔ Quarantine & kill to block the account and kill its live session immediately.
  4. If it's harmless, click ✗ False positive, then Confirm false positive to tune it out. This creates a suppression so that rule stops firing for the scope you chose (this principal, this object, both, or the whole rule).
Changed your mind about a false positive? You can revoke it. Go to Policies & Rules → the Exceptions tab, find the suppression, and click Revoke — the rule starts firing again for that scope. Revoked suppressions are kept in the trail (toggle All to see revoked/expired ones), so there's a full history of what was suppressed and when.
Use ⤓ Export to pull alerts for a report or ticket, and Refresh to fetch the latest.
Step 6

Quarantine risky accounts

The Quarantine screen is your containment console — every held or blocked session and account lives here.

  1. To proactively block a principal, click ⛔ Quarantine account.
  2. Set default containment behaviour with ⛨ Quarantine policy.
  3. On a held session you have two choices:
    • Terminate — kill the live session and keep the account blocked.
    • Release — lift the quarantine; the principal may reconnect.
Terminate vs Release: Terminate = "stop this now, stay blocked." Release = "all clear, let them back in." When in doubt, Terminate and investigate — you can always Release afterwards.
Step 7

Run a vulnerability assessment

Everything up to here watched activity. This step checks the database's own security posture — see Vulnerability assessment for the concept. Open Vulnerability Assessment from the sidebar.

  1. Make sure a database is reachable. A scan runs through a monitoring agent that already has a read-only database login — the same connection used for capture and classification. If you've deployed an agent, you're ready; if not, the page shows an empty state asking you to deploy one first.
  2. Click Run scan. The scan is queued to the agent, which runs the read-only CIS checks on its next poll (~15 seconds) and reports findings back — nothing on the database is changed or exploited.
  3. Read the posture strip. Five KPIs summarise where you stand: Posture score (severity-weighted % of checks passed), Critical open, High open, Open findings (with how many are waived), and Databases scanned with the last-scan time.
  4. Work the findings table. Toggle between Failing (open fails only — the default) and All checks (including passes and n/a). Click any row to expand its evidence (the actual value found), remediation (how to fix it), references, and when it was first/last seen.
  5. A result reads one of three ways:
    • pass — the control is configured securely.
    • fail — a real gap; use the remediation to close it, then re-scan.
    • n/a — the check couldn't run because the read-only reader lacks the privilege to see that setting. Grant the reader the missing view, or accept it's unmeasurable.
  6. Accept a risk you can't fix (admins). On a failing finding, admins see Waive (accept risk) — add a reason and it drops out of the open count (use Remove waiver to reverse). Waiving is an auditable decision, not a way to hide a gap.
Findings feed the Compliance Center: a database with open critical findings, or one not scanned in 90 days, shows up as a compliance gap. Re-scan after remediating so your posture score and compliance status both reflect the fix.
A scan needs a deployed agent with a database login — it can't reach a database you're only monitoring agentlessly through cloud audit logs. No agent, no scan.

Part 3 · Security

How your secrets are protected — encryption & BYOK

SecurEra stores a few sensitive things for you — the read-only cloud keys it uses to discover managed databases, integration secrets, and so on. These are always encrypted before they touch the database. The only question BYOK answers is: who holds the key that can unlock them?

A safe-deposit-box analogy

Your secrets are valuables locked in a box inside a bank (our database). What matters is who keeps the key, and where.

The platform must send its locked data-key to your vault to be opened — it never holds your master key. Switch your key off and the platform is locked out for good (“crypto-shred”).

Where your key can live

The key store is pluggable — the master key can sit in whichever key manager you trust:

  • HashiCorp Vault (Transit) — available now; the key never leaves Vault.
  • AWS KMS, Azure Key Vault, GCP Cloud KMS — available now; keep the key in your own cloud account (true BYOK) — the key material never leaves your KMS.
  • Platform-managed key — the simplest option, managed for you (no separate vault required).
Why the “locked data-key” in the middle? Re-locking a whole box of valuables every time you rotate your key would be slow. So each box has its own small key that does the actual locking, and that small key is what your master key protects. Rotating your master key just re-locks the small key — the valuables are never disturbed. Technical names, if you meet them elsewhere: the small key = DEK (data encryption key); your master key = KEK (key encryption key); “wrapped” = the small key kept locked.

What’s stored where

What BYOK does not change: login passwords are stored as one-way hashes (never encrypted, nothing to key); just-in-time database credentials are issued live by the vault and never stored; and agent-side database logins live on your own database hosts. BYOK is about the secrets the platform keeps for you.

Part 5 · Wrap-up

Best practices for beginners

  • Cover everything. Every unmonitored database is a blind spot — register and deploy widely.
  • Classify early. You can't protect sensitive data you haven't located.
  • Shadow, then enable. New policies go to Monitor first to avoid alert fatigue.
  • Work the queue daily. Acknowledge → investigate → resolve. Don't let alerts pile up.
  • Tune false positives. Every one you dismiss makes the system sharper.
  • Contain fast, ask later. For a real threat, Quarantine & kill first — you can Release if it was fine.
  • Report regularly. Scheduled reports keep auditors (and your future self) happy.
  • Least privilege. Give people the narrowest role that lets them do their job.

Personally Identifiable Information — data that identifies a person.
Policy / Rule
A condition defining risky activity that should raise an alert.
Principal
The user or service account performing database activity.
Quarantine
Isolating/blocking a risky account or session to contain a threat.
Session
A single connection from a principal, from login to logout.
Severity
How urgent an alert is: Critical, High, Medium, or Low.
Shadow / Monitor mode
Running a policy silently — it counts matches but raises no alert.
